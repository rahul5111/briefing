═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Scientists have discovered an exceptionally well-preserved 66-million-year-old bird feather trapped inside a fossilised dinosaur dropping, or coprolite. The specimen was found in 2016 by David DeMar, Jr of the University of Washington while collecting fish fossils in north-eastern Montana’s Hell Creek Formation. DeMar later teamed up with Jingmai O’Connor at the Field Museum in Chicago and other colleagues to analyse the coprolite using micro-CT scans and 3D surface scanning.

The coprolite likely originated from a Nanotyrannus or a young Tyrannosaurus rex. Alongside the feather, the dropping contained two leg-bone fragments measuring 18 and 12 millimetres long, which researchers attributed to hesperornithiformes, a group of flightless, short-winged diving birds related to modern Neornithes. Further analysis revealed the 9.3-millimetre-long primary feather featured a water-repellent structure and a lightweight, spongy core surrounded by a harder outer layer, characteristics typical of modern feathers. The dropping also contained two fluffy body feathers with loosely arranged primitive branches, as well as two rigid, diamond-shaped scales measuring 3 to 4 millimetres long that likely came from a gar eaten by the bird.

According to the researchers, the combination of modern wing feathers and primitive body feathers suggests that hesperornithiformes possessed a middle ground of plumage. While the advanced wing feathers supported diving, the less advanced body feathers likely provided poor insulation, which may explain why these birds failed to survive the harsh winter conditions triggered by the post-asteroid mass extinction 66 million years ago. As Jingmai O’Connor described the find, this is the best feather ever seen in a Mesozoic deposit, noting that it is like a feather you could pick up off the ground.
