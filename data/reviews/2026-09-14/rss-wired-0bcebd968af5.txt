═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

The rapid expansion of data centers and massive power plants by tech companies is being driven by a shift from simple AI chatbots to autonomous AI agents. Unlike standard queries, agents give themselves hundreds of small prompts and can run for hours to execute complex tasks. Recently, OpenAI announced that a swarm of more than 10,000 agents sending 2.7 million messages solved a longstanding math problem, burning through processing power costing tens of millions of dollars. Private AI companies have historically been tight-lipped about environmental metrics, with OpenAI CEO Sam Altman previously comparing single-query resource use to the water required to harvest a single almond, stating that the people eating 12 almonds at a time do not feel they are doing something horrible from a water perspective. Introducing resource-intensive agents complicates these calculations significantly. Climate scientist Zeke Hausfather published a blog post last month estimating that his own agent-heavy daily AI use consumes more energy than needed to power two refrigerators. While this footprint is still relatively small compared to activities like regular flights, tech companies are scaling up infrastructure for a future of ubiquitous agents. Meta recently rolled out a personal AI agent dubbed Muse, designed to maintain a dedicated computer in the cloud for billions of people and integrate with AI glasses later this year, a vision that helps explain massive infrastructure undertakings like the Hyperion project in Louisiana, which will be powered by 10 natural gas plants. Data center developers are installing gas turbines immediately rather than waiting for emerging solutions like small modular nuclear reactors, which face prolonged timelines to commercial operation despite US government pilot projects.
