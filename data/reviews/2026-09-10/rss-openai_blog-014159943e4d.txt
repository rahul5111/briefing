═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Bioengineer César de la Fuente and his cross-disciplinary lab at the University of Pennsylvania are using artificial intelligence, including ChatGPT and Codex, to search the genomes of living and extinct organisms for new antimicrobial molecules. Drug-resistant microbes are a growing global threat, with bacterial antimicrobial resistance associated with about five million deaths in 2021, an annual toll projected to roughly double by 2050. The lab uses deep-learning models alongside ChatGPT and Codex to brainstorm hypotheses, write and refine code, process datasets, analyze results, and bridge gaps between team members from biology, chemistry, computer science, and engineering. While traditional methods of discovering new antimicrobial candidates can take years, AI tools help reduce the initial search phase down from years to hours by scanning vast genome and protein databases to identify functional patterns. De la Fuente uses ChatGPT as a collaborative sounding board and brainstorming partner, though he cautions that researchers must always double-check AI output for accuracy. Because identifying a promising candidate is only the first step, the lab combines AI predictions with ground-truth experiments to confirm that molecules kill target microbes, assess toxicity, and evaluate safety before candidates can advance through preclinical and clinical trials.
