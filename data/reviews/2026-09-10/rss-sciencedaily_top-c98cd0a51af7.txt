═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

On September 9, 2026, the National Radio Astronomy Observatory announced that observations from the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array show interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS contains exceptionally high levels of methanol. Using ALMA's Atacama Compact Array in Chile, researchers observed the comet on several dates in late 2025 as it moved closer to the Sun and sunlight heated its icy surface. Scientists concentrated on the submillimeter signatures of methanol and hydrogen cyanide, measuring methanol-to-HCN ratios of roughly 70 and 120 on two observation dates. These values place 3I/ATLAS among the most methanol-rich comets ever studied, with an abundance exceeding almost all known comets from our own solar system. Nathan Roth, lead author of the research and a professor with American University, shared that observing 3I/ATlass is like taking a fingerprint from another solar system, noting that the details reveal what it is made of and that it is bursting with methanol in a way not typically seen in local comets. Earlier observations with the James Webb Space Telescope had already found a coma dominated by carbon dioxide. The new ALMA data further revealed that while hydrogen cyanide originates mostly from the comet's central nucleus, methanol comes from both the nucleus and tiny icy grains floating within the coma. As temperatures rise, these small grains act like miniature comets by releasing additional methanol into the surrounding gas and dust. This marks the first time researchers have traced the detailed physics of this type of outgassing in an interstellar object. Comet 3I/ATLAS is only the third confirmed object ever observed entering our solar system from interstellar space, following 1I/'Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov. The measurements suggest that the ice inside 3I/ATLAS formed under or was exposed to conditions very different from those that shaped most comets in our solar system, offering insight into how small icy bodies form in other planetary systems.
