═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A new study led by Christina Schädel at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts shows that natural carbon emissions from ecosystems will amplify global warming by 20 to 30 per cent this century. While climate models include several global feedback loops, no single model accounts for all ecosystem feedbacks. The research estimates that these natural processes will heat the planet by an extra 0.2 degrees Celsius to 0.4 degrees Celsius by 2100, adding to the 1 degree Celsius to 2 degrees Celsius of human-driven warming already expected. Even in a scenario where human emissions begin declining today, natural feedbacks will still add 0.2 degrees Celsius of extra warming due to the lag between emissions and temperature impacts. In a scenario where emissions decline only after 2050, the added warming reaches an average of 0.4 degrees Celsius. The study examines four major natural feedbacks. The first involves thawing Arctic permafrost, where microbes decompose ancient organic matter and release carbon dioxide and methane. The second involves wetlands, where warmer water and increased microbial activity release more methane. The third involves ponds and lakes experiencing those same methane-releasing processes. The fourth feedback is driven by hotter air, shorter winters, and drier soils that increase the frequency and intensity of wildfires, resulting in greater carbon dioxide and methane emissions. These emissions are currently left out of carbon budgets. As Tim Lenton at the University of Exeter notes, the findings suggest the world will exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius of global warming further than previously thought, requiring governments to invest more in adaptation measures such as flood barriers, drought-resistant crops, and early-warning systems, and to fully account for all emission sources when planning for the future.
