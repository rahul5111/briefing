═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at the University of California Berkeley published a study on September 11, 2026, in the journal Nature revealing genetic secrets of long-lived bats that could help combat cancer and aging in humans. Lead researcher Juan Manuel Vazquez analyzed eight genomes from the genus Myotis, which includes species with extreme lifespans such as the Brandt's myotis that can survive for 50 years. The findings establish a strong connection between bat longevity and immune function, showing that longer-lived bats possess higher levels of cancer-fighting genes and a unique defense strategy. When exposed to toxic chemicals in laboratory tests, cells from the little brown bat bypassed DNA repair proteins and instead prioritized rapid cell death, mirroring the cancer-resistant survival strategy found in elephants. Vazquez noted that bats evolved to live a long time without getting diseases, stating that researchers can look at bats to try to understand how to improve the human immune system so it does not decline in old age or exhaust its immunity against tumors. The genomic analysis also revealed a substantial overlap between genes associated with aging and those involved in disease defense, particularly regarding interactions with DNA viruses. Unlike humans and other primates who primarily feature genes interacting with RNA viruses like COVID and HIV, Myotis bats possess an unusually large number of genes producing proteins that interact with DNA viruses. Peter Sudmant, a Berkeley associate professor of integrative biology, explained that studying these diverse longevity strategies helps researchers understand the interplay between DNA damage and the immune system. The research was funded by the National Institutes of Health and the National Science Foundation, with Vazquez continuing his investigations at Pennsylvania State University.
