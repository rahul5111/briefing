═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

The San Francisco nonprofit Reflective has published a detailed research road map outlining the experiments, studies, and infrastructure needed to determine whether to deploy solar geoengineering, specifically stratospheric aerosol injection. Incorporated in late 2023, the organization has raised more than $20 million from prominent charities and individuals and distributed roughly $4 million to dozens of research groups. 

According to the report, a coordinated global research effort would take about a decade and cost roughly $370 million. Without coordination, the process would require approximately 20 years and nearly $1.4 billion. 

The road map is structured in sequential phases. The initial foundational knowledge stage would last two to three years and cost between $30 million and $75 million, focusing on computer simulations, lab experiments, and observational tools to establish baseline stratospheric conditions. 

Subsequent phases involve outdoor experiments. A medium-scale research stage would last four to eight years, cost $70 million to $150 million, and use modified aircraft to release 10 metric tons of sulfur dioxide four times across two seasons. A later, larger phase would release 25,000 tons of sulfur dioxide over a single season, running for four to 11 years at a cost of $270 million to $1.1 billion. The plan incorporates formal stage gates to halt research if data reveals worrisome downsides or fails to resolve key uncertainties. 

The proposal enters a contentious scientific debate. Since 2002, hundreds of academics have signed an open letter calling for a ban on outdoor experiments and an international non-use agreement, arguing that such technology cannot be equitably governed and that small-scale trials cannot answer fundamental questions regarding who controls planet-altering interventions. Critics also warn that continued reliance on geoengineering without curbing greenhouse gas emissions creates intergenerational risks, such as termination shock if the system is ever abruptly shut off. Proponents argue the research is necessary to efficiently ascertain whether sunlight reflection is a viable option.
