═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Despite free access to medications, widespread social stigma and discrimination continue to impede efforts to end HIV in Brazil, a nation of approximately 214 million people that holds Latin America's largest population of individuals living with HIV at nearly 1.1 million. According to research from UNAIDS Brazil, more than half of HIV-positive individuals in the country have experienced discrimination. National health data shows that 27 percent of HIV-positive people are diagnosed only after their condition progresses to advanced AIDS. In 2024, more than 9,000 people in Brazil died from AIDS, while government figures indicate that 82 percent of HIV-positive individuals take antiretroviral drugs, falling short of the United Nations target of 95 percent. 

Activists and public health experts attribute the ongoing crisis to persistent fear, a lack of modern education, and high rates of homophobia and transphobia, which lead many to view HIV as an unavoidable death sentence tied to the 1990 death of Brazilian singer Cazuza at age 32. While scientific advancements continue—including Brazil's leadership in real-world studies of the twice-yearly injectable preventative drug lenacapavir—advocates emphasize that social exclusion, fear of judgment, and broken trust in healthcare systems remain primary drivers of preventable deaths and treatment dropouts. 

Fabiana de Oliveira, an activist with the National Network of Women Living with HIV, described the personal toll of this environment, noting that after her diagnosis at age 26, her fiancé of eight years abandoned her and his family banned her from their home. "People are more afraid of how society is going to perceive them than they are of the virus," she said.
