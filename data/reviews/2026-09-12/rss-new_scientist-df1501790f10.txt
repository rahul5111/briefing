═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Scientists have identified a newly discovered two-metre-long reptile as the oldest known representative of the alligator group, having flourished in North American waterways shortly after the asteroid strike that wiped out most dinosaurs 66 million years ago. Researchers from the Denver Museum of Nature and Science recovered hundreds of bones, teeth, and bony skin plates from about 15 individuals of the species, named Mandrasuchus milleri, at the Corral Bluffs fossil site in central Colorado. Radiometrically dated volcanic ash and fossil pollen revealed that the oldest skulls date to 350,000 years after the asteroid impact. The creature featured pointed front teeth for seizing prey and rounded, molar-like back teeth for crushing shellfish, with analysis of 330 anatomical features placing it within the alligatorine subfamily. Several skeletons were found with connected bones, including one inside a potential burrow that may have sheltered the animal from harsh post-impact conditions. Sadie Sherman of the Denver Museum noted that having a large-bodied apex predator thriving so soon after an apocalyptic event is telling, adding that generalist feeding habits and freshwater habitats likely aided survival. As Sherman stated, if you are not picky, you have a much better chance of surviving an extinction event. Adam Cossette at the New York Institute of Technology stated that the species provides rare insight into how alligatorids diversified immediately after the end-Cretaceous extinction.
