═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers analyzing data from the October 2024 gravitational-wave event GW241011 have narrowed the search for black hole impostors by measuring the spin-induced quadrupole moment of the merger's more massive object, or primary. Detected by the LIGO Hanford detector in the U.S. and the Virgo detector in Italy, GW241011 involved two compact objects with masses of about 19.6 and 5.9 solar masses, with the primary exhibiting a dimensionless spin of approximately 0.78 and a high signal-to-noise ratio of about 36 in the detector network. 

Using a method originally proposed in 2017 to test whether compact objects are truly black holes, researchers from the University of Birmingham, the Perimeter Institute for Theoretical Physics, the Canadian Institute for Theoretical Astrophysics, and other institutes combined Bayesian parameter estimation with numerical relativity simulations. Their findings, published in Physical Review Letters, show that the primary object is consistent with a rotating Kerr black hole and rules out certain alternatives, such as rotating boson stars with quartic self-interactions. However, the data does not completely eliminate other sufficiently compact exotic objects, such as those with a compactness greater than or equal to 0.24. 

As co-first author N. V. Krishnendu noted, the work successfully moves from asking whether data is consistent with black holes to placing quantitative constraints on specific alternatives. While the findings currently apply only to the object in this specific binary, future improvements to gravitational-wave detectors ahead of their fifth observing run and planned third-generation facilities are expected to provide enhanced sensitivity and larger source populations to further test the nature of compact objects.
