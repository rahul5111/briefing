═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto have discovered that the immune system appears to trigger labour by transferring energy to the uterus. Led by Stephen Lye, the team found that monocytes from the bloodstream accumulate in the uterine muscle tissue toward the end of pregnancy and transform into macrophages. These macrophages then form tiny tube-like structures that send mitochondria directly into the uterine muscle cells. This influx of mitochondria increases levels of adenosine triphosphate, providing the energy needed for muscle contractions. The process also raises levels of an enzyme that breaks down progesterone locally in the muscle cells, a step that activates the genes required for labour despite circulating progesterone levels remaining high. Understanding this mechanism could lead to therapeutics for preventing preterm birth, which occurs before 37 weeks and increases the risk of conditions such as cerebral palsy, learning difficulties, and asthma. Current tocolytic drugs only delay early labour by a few days, but Lye and his colleagues have tested a broad-spectrum chemokine inhibitor that blocked preterm birth in four macaques by stopping the initial recruitment of monocyte immune cells. The researchers are also studying blood samples from patients showing signs of early labour to identify who is truly at risk and ultimately develop a blood test to predict when full-term labour will begin.
