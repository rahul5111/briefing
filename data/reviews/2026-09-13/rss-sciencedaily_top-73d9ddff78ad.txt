═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University, the University of Pittsburgh, and the University of Washington published a study in Science on September 13, 2026, revealing that the three-dimensional organization of the genome is disrupted in brain cells affected by Alzheimer's disease. The team examined postmortem prefrontal cortex tissue samples from individuals with and without Alzheimer's using a technique called GAGE-seq, spatial transcriptomic maps, and a deep learning model named Hicformer. 

The investigation showed that large sections of the genome normally organized into distinct active and inactive compartments become less sharply defined in Alzheimer's cells, a pattern the researchers call increased compartment mingling. Affected cells displayed fewer interactions between nearby genome sections, more contacts between distant regions, and weaker interactions between genes and nearby regulatory elements. These structural shifts were linked to reduced gene activity in programs involving neurons and synapses, alterations in metabolism and cellular stress responses, and senescence-related programs in microglia.

"Alzheimer's disease cannot be understood one layer at a time," said Jian Ma, the Ray and Stephanie Lane Professor of Computational Biology who led the study. Hansruedi Mathys, assistant professor of neurobiology at the University of Pittsburgh, noted that the findings establish higher-order chromatin alterations as part of the molecular pathology associated with the disease, which currently affects seven million Americans. The research was supported by grants from the National Institutes of Health.
