═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

New research reveals that the distant star AT 2016blu, long suspected of preparing to explode as a supernova, is actually a supernova impostor driven by interactions with a previously unseen binary companion. Located about 29 million light-years or 170 trillion miles away, the primary star is a luminous blue variable about 33 times as massive as the sun. Discovered in 2012, the star has erupted 27 times in quasiperiodic bursts roughly every 113 days. 

University of Virginia astronomer Mojgan Aghakhanloo led a research team that utilized new X-ray observations alongside archival data to uncover the mechanism behind the outbursts. The findings are detailed in a forthcoming paper in The Astrophysical Journal titled AT 2016blu: Accretion-Powered Outbursts in a Luminous Blue Variable and Compact Object Binary, currently available on arXiv.org. 

By coordinating observations with multiple telescopes and more than 30 amateur astronomers, the research team successfully scheduled observations during the star's peak outburst in March 2026. This effort included a Target of Opportunity request that interrupted the schedule of the Chandra X-ray Observatory. Chandra detected an X-ray source during the predicted outburst, whereas archival observations from 2001 to 2002 showed no X-rays. The measured luminosity and calculated mass accretion rate indicate that the outbursts are caused by intermittent accretion onto a compact object, such as a black hole or neutron star. 

As the authors write, "AT 2016blu represents the first known LBV system with a compact companion." Because the system consists of a massive star and a compact companion, it is classified as a high-mass X-ray binary. Aghakhanloo has secured additional Chandra observing time to study other supernova impostors and determine whether they behave in the same way. Studying these systems helps advance understanding of massive stars, which forge and distribute heavy elements throughout the universe.
