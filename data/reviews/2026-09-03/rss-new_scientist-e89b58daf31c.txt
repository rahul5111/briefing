═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center found that psilocybin may prevent chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, a common and debilitating nerve damage side effect that affects about 75 per cent of cancer patients undergoing the treatment. The condition occurs when chemotherapy drugs disrupt the transport of energy-supplying mitochondria to nerve endings, causing them to wear out and retract. In tests on nerve samples from mice and humans, psilocybin maintained this energy supply, resulting in less nerve damage. 

In experiments, mice with abdominal tumours received two abdominal injections of psilocybin, each equivalent to a 25-milligram dose in humans, spaced one week apart, followed by daily injections of the chemotherapy drug cisplatin for one week. Pain-sensitivity tests showed that mice receiving psilocybin before cisplatin responded similarly to a control group that received neither substance, whereas mice receiving cisplatin without psilocybin showed heightened sensitivity. Analysis revealed that the active ingredient psilocin activates the 5HT2A receptor, sending molecular signals that prevent chemotherapy-related disruption of mitochondrial transport. 

Researchers caution that patients should not take psilocybin at home, as it has not been proven safe or effective during chemotherapy and can interact dangerously with cancer medications. A randomised controlled trial involving about 80 people with breast, colorectal, or head and neck cancer is scheduled to begin in November, with results expected in about five years. 

As Moran Amit at MD Anderson noted, no drug had previously been shown to prevent this condition, which currently lacks effective treatments beyond moderate pain relief from duloxetine. Maria Maiaru at the University of Reading, who was not involved in the research, stated that if the findings are replicated in clinical trials, patients could avoid long-lasting sensory problems and chronic pain, while potentially requiring fewer chemotherapy dose reductions or interruptions to improve outcomes.
