═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A blanket of toxic haze is enveloping parts of Indonesia and spreading into Malaysia, Singapore, and the Philippines due to weeks-long wildfires burning across Sumatra and Indonesian Borneo since mid-July. The smog has exposed millions of people to pollution levels more than 15 times the World Health Organization recommendation, fueled by a super El Niño intensifying drought conditions. Between July and August, Indonesia's Health Ministry recorded over 50,000 respiratory infection cases linked to the fires, with more than 12,000 affecting children under five. Malaysia has reported rising cases of asthma and conjunctivitis, while residents in the Philippine island province of Palawan reported mild headaches and itchy eyes, nose, and throat. The severity of the crisis is driven by both drought and human activity, including decades of land-use changes, drained peatlands, and industrial plantations. The fires are burning through one of the world's largest tropical peatland ecosystems, which Dr Nisa Novita of Yayasan Konservasi Alam Nusantara warned can become carbon bombs when drained or burned. Professor Bambang Hero Saharjo of Institut Pertanian Bogor University cited research warning that this year's El Niño could burn an area as large as 11 million hectares, comparable to Indonesia's severe 1997 to 1998 wildfire season. Indonesia's forestry minister Raja Juli Antoni stated earlier this week that the battle to extinguish the fires is entering a critical phase, noting that September marks the peak of El Niño. Rizma Nur Fatimah, a 19-year-old teaching assistant in South Kalimantan, described the conditions, saying that inside her house with everything closed, the haze still got in and became so thick you could not see your own feet.
