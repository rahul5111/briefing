═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A new study published in the American Journal of Human Genetics shows that almost all of the world's top computer software used to predict dangerous DNA mutations can confuse rare mutations with harmful ones. A research team led by Dr. Donate Weghorn at the Center for Genomic Regulation in Barcelona tested 50 prediction tools against 13.5 million mutations spread across 6,659 human genes. The programs evaluated included AlphaMissense, built by Google DeepMind, as well as EVE and popEVE, which were co-developed by other research groups at the Center for Genomic Regulation. The study found that almost all of the programs overestimate the potential harm of mutations that are less likely to occur than average and underestimate the effect of more likely mutations. This occurs because the software assumes that DNA spots remaining unchanged over millions of years matter and that changing them is harmful, failing to account for regional differences in how mutation-prone the genome is. For example, the starting points of genes are 35 percent more prone to mutations than other regions. The researchers found that mutations in genes for DNA repair, cilia, and sperm function are likely being overcalled as dangerous, while genes linked to intellectual disability and single-parent conditions are likely being undercalled. As Dr. Weghorn stated, both would be bad. To test the tools, the team used experimental data from the laboratory of Ben Lehner, where researchers physically made hundreds of thousands of mutations across 500 human protein fragments and measured the damage. The authors of the study state that a practical fix would involve modifying the computer software to include information from maps showing where the human genome is more or less mutation-prone.
