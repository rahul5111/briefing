═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

AI-assisted incident response tools are successfully handling routine alerts and fixes, but human engineers are losing touch with how their systems behave. As automation resolves everyday issues, responders get less practice with the systems they oversee. This creates what human-factors researcher Lisanne Bainbridge described in 1983 as the ironies of automation, where automation reduces routine practice while leaving humans responsible for abnormal situations. While AI will likely lower mean time to resolution for routine problems, resolution times for complex incidents may spike as engineers struggle to investigate unfamiliar failures. The aviation industry faces a similar dynamic where automation handles routine flight, yet pilots must manage rare emergencies. To maintain necessary skills, commercial pilots complete recurrent training and proficiency checks every six months under Federal Aviation Administration rules. Software engineering teams face a similar need to prevent comprehension debt, the growing gap between system mechanics and responder understanding. Companies like Rootly and Uptime Labs are addressing this by running realistic incident simulations where engineers practice troubleshooting, coordination, and communication during mock outages. Passive instruction and watching AI agents explain their diagnostic steps are not substitutes for hands-on practice. Engineering teams must use tabletop exercises, chaos engineering, and simulations to maintain their skills so responders remain prepared when automation fails.
