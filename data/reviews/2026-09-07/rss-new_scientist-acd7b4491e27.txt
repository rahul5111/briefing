═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A super-sized El Niño weather pattern in the Pacific Ocean is set to cause a devastating drought in the Amazon, threatening to fuel huge wildfires in a region scientists warn is approaching a climate tipping point. El Niño is a natural phenomenon where sea-surface temperatures in the tropical Pacific rise above normal due to shifts in trade winds, pushing up global temperatures and causing extreme weather. Scientists monitoring the current development say it is set to be the most intense in modern history by a large margin, with ocean temperatures well above normal and still rising. Adam Scaife at the UK Met Office notes that very strong El Niños concentrate heat close to South America's coast, leading to intense regional impacts. The Met Office's latest forecasts show a terribly dangerous rainfall anomaly developing over the Amazon by January, alongside hotter air temperatures. Tim Li at the University of Hawaii warns that the Amazon is set to suffer the most extreme El Niño-induced drought on record. Rainfall over the Amazon during June, July, and August was already well below average, with Li stating that the rainfall anomaly in 2026 during the summertime is already much stronger than previous large events. Air temperatures in the region are also much higher than normal, creating a positive feedback loop where reduced moisture and evaporation lead to even warmer temperatures. Severe droughts in the Amazon have historically had dire consequences; during the 2015-16 El Niño event, extreme drought fuelled wildfires that destroyed around 2.5 billion trees and plants and emitted 495 million tonnes of carbon dioxide, according to research led by the University of Oxford. Repeated and severe droughts push the rainforest closer to a tipping point where the ecosystem could collapse and be replaced by savannah, threatening a globally significant biodiversity hotspot that plays a key role in removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
