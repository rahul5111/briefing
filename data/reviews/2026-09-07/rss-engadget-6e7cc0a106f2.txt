1. Passive Ethernet splitters limit maximum connection speeds to 10Mbps and 100Mbps.
2. Using a passive Ethernet splitter setup actually requires purchasing two separate splitter units.
3. Active Ethernet splitters contain dedicated switching hardware and require an external power input.
4. Network switches offer anywhere from a handful to dozens of RJ45 ports for expansion.
5. Inexpensive network switches support Gigabit Ethernet while higher-end models offer 2.5Gbps ports.

═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

When connecting multiple devices to the internet off a single Ethernet cable, choosing between a splitter and a switch depends on hardware capabilities, speed requirements, and port count. Modern Ethernet cables use four twisted pairs of copper wires to achieve gigabit speeds. Passive Ethernet splitters divide these pairs into two separate two-pair connections, allowing two devices to connect, but they are limited to older 10Mbps and 100Mbps transmission standards. Furthermore, passive splitters require using two units—one at each end of the cable run—because a router cannot communicate with two different devices through a single physical RJ45 port. Active or powered Ethernet splitters contain dedicated switching hardware and require a power input, allowing them to manage traffic and support full gigabit bandwidth while only requiring a single unit at the endpoint. For scenarios requiring multiple ports from a single cable run, a conventional network switch is the most versatile solution. Switches offer anywhere from a handful to dozens of RJ45 ports for future expansion, with inexpensive unmanaged models supporting Gigabit Ethernet and higher-end consumer versions offering 2.5Gbps ports. This speed distinction matters for local tasks like backing up data through network-attached storage or serving media through Plex, provided the connected devices also support 2.5Gbps speeds. For home settings, simple unmanaged switches from brands like Netgear and TP-Link provide plug-and-play functionality, letting users connect one port to a router while using the rest for devices like a TV.
