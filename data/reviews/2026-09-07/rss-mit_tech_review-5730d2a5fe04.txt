═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

The conflict in Iran and resulting natural gas spikes have severely disrupted the global fertilizer market, driving costs up and threatening farmers with elevated prices through at least 2028. Natural gas serves as both an energy source and a chemical input for ammonia, connecting fertilizer directly to fossil fuel fluctuations. Additionally, about one-third of global seaborne fertilizer trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz, which has been effectively closed to commercial traffic due to the conflict. In April, the price of urea climbed above $850 per metric ton, marking an 80% increase from pre-conflict levels and the highest point since 2022. The crisis has also damaged or shut down 31 ammonia plants in the Middle East, compounding earlier damage to 20 plants in Russia. Travis Frey, chief technology officer of Pivot Bio, describes the situation by saying, there is just this out-of-control supply chain that is a lot more volatile than it has ever been. Because farmers operate on paper-thin margins, these spikes pose a severe threat to their businesses and ultimately push food prices higher for consumers. In response, biotech alternatives that use microbes to provide nitrogen are seeing increased attention. Companies like Pivot Bio and Switch Bioworks are producing microbe-based fertilizers that avoid natural gas inputs and are not subject to the same price spikes, though current technology can only replace a fraction of traditional synthetic fertilizers. Tim Schnabel, founder and CEO of Switch Bioworks, notes the urgency of the shift, stating that we cannot keep doing it like this and that there is no way we can build a society where the basis of the food chain depends on fossil fuels.
