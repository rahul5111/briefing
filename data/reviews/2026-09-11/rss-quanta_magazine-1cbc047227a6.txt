═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

For decades, micropaleontologists have puzzled over why certain species of planktonic foraminifera, single-celled marine organisms with hard shells dating back 56 million years, suddenly switch their spiral shell-coiling direction globally all at once. 

While a 1959 study by marine geologist David Ericson linked left-coiling shells in the species Neogloboquadrina pachyderma to ice ages and temperature, subsequent genetic work and global core sampling discredited the temperature hypothesis. Recently, a team led by University College London micropaleontologist Bridget Wade synthesized five decades of data spanning multiple ocean basins and climate belts. The analysis revealed that several species flipped their coiling directions simultaneously across the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific oceans. For example, Globorotalia scitula shifted from mixed to left-handed coiling 15 million years ago, and then to right-handed 10 million years ago. Pulleniatina obliquiloculata coiled almost exclusively to the right for the past 860,000 years, but prior to that experienced rapid, globally synchronized flips every few thousand years.

The new study hypothesizes that these sudden, worldwide shifts are not adaptive responses to climate change, but accidental markers of significant evolutionary events. Researchers suggest that a new genetic variant or cryptic species develops a broad fitness advantage and rapidly spreads across global ocean basins, much like a viral variant, bringing a specific coiling direction to dominance and leaving a permanent record in the seafloor sediment. 

“It seems truly puzzling that a species could exist for millions of years coiling one way, and then suddenly reverse, for no apparent reason,” the authors wrote. 

While the flips appear instantaneous in the geological record, scientists note the actual spread likely unfolded over 1,000 years or more, accelerated by global ocean circulation. Because other marine organisms lack such distinct fossil markers, these foraminifera shells offer a rare window into how evolutionary traits sweep across the globe.
