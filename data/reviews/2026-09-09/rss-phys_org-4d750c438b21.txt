═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

New research published in Frontiers in Ethology details how GPS collars were used to map the movements of African lions and spotted hyenas, overturning the long-held assumption that hyenas simply shadow lions for food scraps. Lead author Dr. Nancy Barker of the University of KwaZulu-Natal and her colleagues tracked 17 lions and 14 spotted hyenas over a four-year period across Etosha National Park in Namibia and Chobe National Park and the adjoining Linyanti Conservancy in Botswana. By analyzing GPS fixes taken every five minutes during peak activity hours, the researchers identified 23 pairs of simultaneously tracked animals, including 10 lion-hyena pairs. The data revealed that spotted hyenas consistently move away from lions, whereas male lions tend to move toward hyenas. Barker noted that while male lions actively approach hyenas, females show more ambivalence depending on the presence of other pride members. As Barker stated, our GPS collar data show that African lions and spotted hyenas leave a clear, measurable signature in the way they move around one another. Because the hyenas retreat signal was consistent across three independent statistical tests, researchers concluded it reflects a repeatable behavioral pattern of interference competition and asymmetric coordination.
