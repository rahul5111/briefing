═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Archaeologists working at Bianfu cave in Yunnan province, China, have uncovered the first clear evidence of how Denisovans lived in south-west China, revealing that the ancient human group inhabited the region for more than 100,000 years. Following a rescue excavation launched in the summer of 2019 after local workers spotted fossils, researchers dug three trenches to a depth of 6.6 metres. They unearthed over 64,000 bone fragments and 1,366 stone artefacts. Molecular analysis of proteins extracted from four teeth and three bone fragments confirmed they belonged to at least four individual Denisovans, as the specimens contained protein variants unique to the group without any specific to Homo sapiens or Neanderthals. Direct and sediment dating placed the bones between 134,000 and 167,000 years old, while the artefacts span a timeframe from 70,000 to 190,000 years ago. The stone tools, including scrapers, choppers, and 135 flakes, were relatively simple compared to those made by contemporaneous Neanderthals. However, the site yielded 1,654 identifiable animal bones showing butchery marks, predominantly from deer and bovids in prime condition, along with rhinoceroses, hyenas, and bamboo rats. Pollen evidence indicates the cave was situated in a temperate ecosystem featuring a mix of conifer forest and steppe. For the first time, as archaeologist Hao Li at the Institute of Tibetan Plateau Research in Beijing notes, we can know their technology and subsistence strategy.
