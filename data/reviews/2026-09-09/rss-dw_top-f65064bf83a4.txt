═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Following weeks of national consultations involving around 4,000 delegates in Addis Ababa, recommendations have emerged to potentially merge the offices of Ethiopia's prime minister and president into a single, stronger presidential system. Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, who did not participate in the meetings, could benefit significantly from the shift given his massive majority won in June's parliamentary elections. 

Supporters argue a directly elected president representing the country as a whole could help unite an ethnically and regionally fragmented society. However, civil rights activist and political commentator Befeqadu Hailu warned that presidential systems are more prone to descending into dictatorship, noting that while the current parliament has procedural legitimacy to enact constitutional changes, the institutions lack broad popular legitimacy. Opposition parties and influential armed groups boycotted or were absent from the dialogue process, leading British journalist and Ethiopia expert Martin Plaut to describe the initiative as merely a rubber-stamping exercise for the prime minister. Plaut added that the reform largely formalizes the power Abiy already holds since taking office in 2018, and views the structural change as a superficial fix that fails to address underlying armed conflicts in Oromia and Amhara, unresolved issues from the Tigray war, and persistent tensions between the central government and Ethiopia's more than 80 ethnic groups.
