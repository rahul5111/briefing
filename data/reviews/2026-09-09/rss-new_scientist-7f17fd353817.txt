═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A new study published in Human Reproduction Open has linked maternal use of paracetamol during early pregnancy to smaller reproductive organs in infants. Researchers examined 685 pregnant women recruited during their first trimester who reported medicine use every two weeks. Among 302 infant daughters examined at roughly three months of age, those exposed to the painkiller as fetuses had, on average, 40 per cent smaller ovaries by volume, 13 per cent smaller uteruses by volume, and 23 per cent fewer ovarian follicles compared to unexposed infants. Higher doses were associated with a larger effect, though no participants exceeded the recommended daily maximum of 4000 milligrams. The team also observed a reduction in average testicular volume among infant boys born to the cohort, and noted that data from 1210 girls born in Copenhagen between 1997 and 2002 showed a link between fetal paracetamol exposure and smaller ovaries during adolescence. Margit Bistrup Fischer at Copenhagen University Hospital–Rigshospitalet stated that while the findings do not prove causation, pregnant women should have a dialogue about when the drug is actually necessary and consider non-pharmacological alternatives for mild discomfort. Other experts cautioned that the findings do not establish a causal relationship and could stem from chance, underlying conditions, or confounding factors. Researchers emphasized that medical advice regarding paracetamol use for high fevers or strong pain remains unchanged, and it is still unclear whether infant organ size reductions affect fertility in adulthood.
