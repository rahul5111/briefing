═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at the Boyce Thompson Institute and Cornell University have developed an artificial intelligence tool called AIMe, or AI Molecule Explorer, to identify and map thousands of small molecules produced by the human body and gut microbiome that previously could not be matched to any known structure. Currently, more than 80% of compounds found in biological samples go unidentified because experimental reference libraries cover less than 1% of known compounds. 

Led by chemistry professor Frank Schroeder and computing professor Carla Gomes, the research team used neuro-symbolic AI to predict and organize mass spectra for more than 100 million known small organic molecules from PubChem. The resulting resource, called MS2KOSMOS, contains more than 800 million predicted spectra, expanding searchable chemical space roughly a thousandfold. At the core of the tool is a model called DeepMS2Reasoner, which simulates how molecules fragment inside a mass spectrometer. Carla Gomes explained that the model builds fragmentation pathways step by step by using symbolic chemical rules to enumerate physically plausible steps and a neural network to assign likelihoods.

When applied to a comparative metabolomics dataset from mice with and without gut microbiota, AIMe identified close spectral matches and structural candidates for about a third of 111 unidentified compounds and mapped the rest to related molecular neighborhoods. This led to the discovery of a structurally unusual macrocyclic polyamine, a ring-shaped variant confirmed by synthesis and subsequently found in 57 of 99 human fecal samples examined. Polyamines sit at the intersection of diet, the gut microbiome, and immune function. Furthermore, when tested at a repository scale on more than 7 million spectral clusters from the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking database, AIMe returned putative annotations for approximately 2.69 million clusters, compared to roughly 416,000 annotated through prior efforts.

The study, authored by Utku U. Acikalin and colleagues, was published as a preprint in bioRxiv in 2026.
