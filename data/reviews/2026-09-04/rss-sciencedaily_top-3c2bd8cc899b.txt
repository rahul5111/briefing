═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A study published on September 4, 2026, in Neuron by researchers from the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbruecke, Charité -- Universitätsmedizin Berlin, and other institutions shows that slow breathing with an extended exhalation phase makes people more willing to take risks by altering heart activity and increasing the brain's sensitivity to rewards. 

Led by Professor Soyoung Q Park, the study examined 41 healthy participants who made risky choices while following visual breathing instructions at either their normal pace or a slowed pace with a two to eight inhale-exhale ratio. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging alongside monitoring of breathing, heart activity, skin conductance, and pupil responses, the researchers measured physical and neurological reactions. 

The results demonstrated that extended exhalation slowed the heart rate and shifted choices toward greater risk-taking by increasing attention to possible rewards, while responses to potential losses remained similar. The intervention also produced stronger activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and the precuneus, brain regions involved in reward processing and heart rate variability regulation. Lead author Wenhao Huang noted that the interplay between breathing and cardiac dynamics makes the brain more receptive to rewards. 

As stated by Soyoung Q Park, our decisions are rarely determined solely by external information, and judgment emerges from the interplay between cognitive processes and the current bodily state. Because controlled breathing is simple and inexpensive, the researchers suggest it could serve as a tool for everyday self-regulation and a supportive, non-pharmacological approach for clinical conditions like anxiety disorders or depression. Future studies will explore whether breathing techniques can influence food-related decisions in clinical populations, including people with overweight.
