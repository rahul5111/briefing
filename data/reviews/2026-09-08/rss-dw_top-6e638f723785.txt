═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Fifty years after the death of revolutionary leader Mao Zedong on September 9, 1976, his legacy continues to shape China. While Mao left behind immense social trauma from successive political campaigns, his institutional core principle that the party leads everything has endured, ensuring the Chinese Communist Party can concentrate resources to accomplish major tasks at all costs. Zhang Lun, a professor at Cergy-Paris University, stated that Mao's most fundamental political legacy is an authoritarian and highly centralized system, and that China's greatest challenge has been that its de-Maoization process was never fully completed.

Under current leader Xi Jinping, political concepts associated with the Mao era, such as ideological unity and struggle, have attracted attention, with Xi abolishing presidential term limits and strengthening personal authority. However, experts caution against equating the two leaders. Felix Wemheuer, professor of modern China studies at the University of Cologne, noted that Xi lacks Mao's historical authority and personal charisma, and unlike Mao, who was an anti-bureaucratic populist capable of mobilizing the masses against the party-state, Xi is a party man who governs strictly through institutions. University of Zurich professor Jean Christopher Mittelstaedt pointed out that Xi remains a product of the system Mao created. While contemporary China operates a dynamic state capitalism far removed from Mao-era economics, authoritarian rule, political rigidity, and a weak rule of law remain significant continuities.

Additionally, recurring waves of online Mao nostalgia reflect a longing for an imagined era without social inequality. Mittelstaedt noted that the real Mao era was marked by rigid hierarchies and was in many respects more unequal than China today. Because public criticism, independent trade unions, free media, and opposition parties are tightly restricted, citizens use Mao's image and revolutionary language as a safe historical symbol to criticize contemporary corruption, inequality, and abuses of power. Mittelstaedt described this as a double-edged sword, where Mao remains a symbol of regime legitimacy while his language can be turned into a standard to measure and criticize contemporary reality.
