═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at the University of Maryland have discovered a powerful new antivenom approach by utilizing toxin-blocking proteins naturally found in rattlesnake blood. Published on September 8, 2026, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the study was led by Distinguished University Professor of Biology Sean B. Carroll and co-authored by researchers including Elda Sánchez, director of the National Natural Toxins Research Center at Texas A&M University-Kingsville. While individual proteins from the blood could counter certain venom effects, they could not prevent death on their own. However, when researchers combined several of these FETUA proteins, the resulting mixtures proved about 10 times more potent in laboratory tests than current sheep-derived commercial rattlesnake antivenom. The optimized combinations completely neutralized the lethal effects of rattlesnake venom and provided broad protection against venom from multiple viper species separated by millions of years of evolution. The findings point toward a new generation of nature-inspired, lab-produced snakebite treatments that could be safer, less expensive, and easier to manufacture on a large scale than traditional antivenoms derived from animals. According to the World Health Organization, venomous snakes kill between 80,000 and 140,000 people annually, leaving hundreds of thousands of survivors with permanent disabilities, making effective antivenom a critical global health need. Carroll expects that the first commercial applications of the research could appear in veterinary medicine, with human treatments following later. As Carroll stated, "This is one of those great stories when nature has already solved a problem we've been grappling with for decades."
