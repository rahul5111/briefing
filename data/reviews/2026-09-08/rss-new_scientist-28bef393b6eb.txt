═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Tristan Buckmaster at New York University and Levent Alpöge at AI company Anthropic have announced three important steps toward solving the Navier-Stokes equations, one of the world's six remaining Millennium Problems carrying a one million dollar prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The Navier-Stokes equations model fluid flow for aircraft wings, blood flow, and space rockets, but can break down and output nonsense. 

Writing in a document uploaded to his website, Buckmaster and Alpöge claim three results relating to close cousins of Navier-Stokes, specifically the Boussinesq approximation and the Euler equations, though the findings are not yet generalized to the wider Navier-Stokes problem. Two of the results have been published alongside Lean formalisation, a process that converts mathematical theory into computer code for rigorous error checking, while the third awaits a finished formalisation. The work builds on previous research by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa using substantial help from large language models from Anthropic and OpenAI. 

Describing the integration of artificial intelligence, Buckmaster said, This is a Deep Blue-Kasparov moment. David Silvester at the University of Manchester noted that the work on Euler equations shows spontaneous turbulence can appear, and suggested the current findings alone might be enough for the pair to claim the Millennium prize, though expanding it to Navier-Stokes will not be trivial. Terence Tao at the University of California, Los Angeles, stated he would not be surprised if an extension could be achieved by pouring an enormous amount of compute and AI assistance at the task. Camilla Nobili at the University of Surrey expressed confidence in the approach, though she believes running the same technique on Navier-Stokes will require more than just that. Silvester added that a working result will not change practical applications because existing fluid dynamics computer models are already exceptionally good.
