═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A new study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences reveals that ancient melons in East Asia likely originated from South Asia rather than developing from wild Chinese plants, and that they lacked the sweetness of modern dessert melons. Researchers from Washington University in St. Louis analyzed ancient DNA extracted from waterlogged melon seeds discovered during the 2024 excavation of the Shuomen Gugang medieval port in Wenzhou, China. The site dates to the Song dynasty, circa 960 to 1279. The genomic analysis showed that the historical melons likely featured light-green flesh and yellow peel, making them closer to vegetables or mildly sweet fruit rather than high-sugar dessert crops. Regarding how the plants arrived, researchers point to two potential historical paths: a northern Silk Road or a southern Tea Horse Road. Current archaeological evidence favors the southern route because the earliest melon remains in China come from Han and pre-Han royal tombs in the south, predating northern Silk Road sites by roughly 800 to 1,000 years. The study also notes a connection between the melons and local aesthetics. The light-green flesh color likely resonated with Song dynasty preferences for jade and was mirrored in locally crafted, melon-shaped green celadon ceramics. As co-author Xinyi Liu stated, taken together, this represents a rare and compelling case of cultural-genetic symbiosis, reflected in the parallel historical selection of melon traits and celadon aesthetics.
