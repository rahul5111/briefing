Somali piracy off the Horn of Africa has returned to levels unseen in a decade, driven by regional instability and shipping disruptions linked to the US-Israeli war against Iran and Houthi attacks in the Red Sea. Since late April, there have been eight hijackings, including the August capture of the Cameroon-flagged Lutuf carrying military equipment for Turkey. The Lutuf was released two weeks later following a joint Turkish-Somali operation that destroyed four pirate vessels, though local officials confirmed a two million five hundred thousand dollar ransom was also paid. 

Global navies have diverted resources from the Horn of Africa to protect waterways threatened by Iranian and Houthi campaigns, while about 70 percent of maritime traffic previously passing through the Red Sea is now rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope, bringing commercial ships closer to the Somali coastline. Matthew Reisener of the Center for Maritime Security stated that these conflicts have created openings for opportunistic pirates, writing that global navies have diverted resources and these conflicts have drawn maritime traffic closer to the coastline, creating openings for opportunistic pirates to target commercial ships. 

Pirate groups are using previously seized vessels as motherships and employing SpaceX Starlink satellite internet for tracking and communications. The resurgence has injected cash into local economies such as Galkayo, while experts question whether there is international appetite or capacity for a counter-piracy naval mission on the scale of the one deployed in 2011 and 2012, when annual operations exceeded one billion dollars.
