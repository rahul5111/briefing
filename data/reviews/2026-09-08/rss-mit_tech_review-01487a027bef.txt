═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Hannah Earley, 31, is the cofounder and chief technology officer of Vaire Computing, a startup developing chips that recycle energy usually lost as heat through a method called reversible computing. Traditional chips erase unneeded information and dissipate energy as heat, while reversible circuits retain intermediate steps so calculations can run backward to recover energy. 

Although the concept was proposed over 50 years ago, Earley designed a patent-pending microscopic resonator, describing it as a glorified pendulum that stores recovered energy for reuse. Last year, Vaire announced a breakthrough by producing a chip with a resonator that recovered more energy than it consumed. 

Earley began programming around age nine and later pursued a PhD at the University of Cambridge under computational biologist Gos Micklem. Her doctoral research focused on the physical limits of computation and software for reversible programs. In 2021, she completed her degree and cofounded Vaire with technology entrepreneur and investor Rodolfo Rosini. The startup has raised more than $12 million and hired reversible computing pioneer Michael Frank as a senior scientist. 

Igor Markov, a researcher in electronic design automation and former University of Michigan professor, notes the technology remains at an early stage and requires increasingly realistic demonstrations to secure commercial support. Vaire's next challenge is adapting the new chip design to fit into standard manufacturing systems and familiar devices. Earley aims to rebuild computers from the ground up to improve energy efficiency in data centers, laptops, and phones.

Her doctoral research focused on the physical limits of computation and building software to turn ordinary programs into reversible ones. In 2021, she completed her degree and cofounded Vaire with technology entrepreneur and investor Rodolfo Rosini. The startup has raised more than $12 million and hired reversible computing pioneer Michael Frank as a senior scientist. 

Igor Markov, a researcher in electronic design automation and former University of Michigan professor, notes the technology remains at an early stage and requires increasingly realistic demonstrations to secure commercial support. Vaire's next challenge is making their drastically different chip fit into familiar devices and manufacturing systems. Ultimately, Earley aims to rebuild computers from the ground up with an eye toward reversibility to make data centers, laptops, and phones much more energy efficient.
