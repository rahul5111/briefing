═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Almost two years after the overthrow of the regime of former Syrian dictator Bashar Assad, a Damascus court sentenced Assad and other high-ranking regime members to death in absentia in mid-August. Assad was found guilty of premeditated murder, torture, and depriving individuals of liberty, which the court classified as war crimes and crimes against humanity. Assad currently lives in Russia, where he fled after his government fell, and many senior officials are also there or hiding elsewhere. Although Syria and Russia agreed on an extradition treaty in 2023, observers consider it unlikely that Russia will return Assad, and the treaty stipulates that the likelihood of the death penalty can also prevent extradition. In August, Syria's Deputy Justice Minister Mustafa al-Qassem stated the country established a commission to cooperate with Interpol to speed up legal proceedings for fugitive suspects.

Data from the Syrian Network for Human Rights indicates the scale of the crimes, showing over 234,805 civilians were killed during the civil war, with at least 202,000 deaths attributed to the Assad regime and its allies. The network also documented at least 181,312 cases of arbitrary arrest and enforced disappearance, 45,339 deaths resulting from torture, and 217 chemical weapons attacks by the Assad regime. The new Syrian government has established two commissions for transitional justice, including the National Commission for Missing Persons, which launched a platform called Athar in late August for families of the missing, and the National Commission for Transitional Justice, established in May 2025, which has so far addressed only crimes committed by the Assad regime. Human rights advocates note that the current process falls short because it has not yet investigated crimes committed by militias or the Islamic State, and systematic state documentation of violent crimes is only just beginning. For survivors like Huda Khayti, a women's rights activist who survived a 2013 chemical weapons attack in east Ghouta, the sentences remain largely symbolic without actual accountability, and true transitional justice requires victims to participate fully in every detail of the process while receiving protection.
