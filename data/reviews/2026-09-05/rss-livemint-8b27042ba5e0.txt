═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

The Arctic is emerging as a competitive arena for global powers driven by strategic locations, military value, shipping routes, natural resources, and climate change. Russia holds the largest Arctic presence, controlling about half the land and maritime exclusive economic zone north of the Arctic Circle, and accounting for roughly two-thirds of the Arctic GDP. According to Simons Foundation research, Russia maintains 30 military sites in the wider Arctic alongside hundreds of defense installations, with modernised nuclear-powered submarines and upgraded radar, drone, and missile capabilities. 

Post-Cold War cooperation, including the 1996 establishment of the Arctic Council and participation in the Arctic Chiefs of Defense Forum, ended following Russia's 2014 Crimea annexation and 2022 Ukraine invasion. The accession of Finland and Sweden to NATO in 2023 and 2024 left the region broadly divided between Russia and NATO. Meanwhile, US President Donald Trump has repeatedly argued that Greenland, an autonomous Danish territory, should become part of the US because Denmark lacks the military strength to protect it from Russia and China. 

China, which declared itself a near-Arctic state in 2018, is pursuing a polar silk road to leverage melting ice for shorter trade routes between Asia and Europe, and conducted a joint Arctic patrol with Russia in 2024. Climate change is warming the region at roughly four times the global average, opening the Northern Sea Route, which can cut transit times between Asia and Europe to about two weeks, and the Northwest Passage, which saw 41 through sailings in 2023. Additionally, Greenland's Geological Survey notes the island holds potential deposits of coal, copper, gold, rare-earth elements, and zinc, though harsh conditions make extraction difficult and expensive.
