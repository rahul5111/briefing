In July 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy selected 278 projects for its Genesis Mission to integrate artificial intelligence into scientific research. One funded project involves using AI to help physicists control experimental aspects of the Mu2e experiment at the Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory near Chicago, searching for a rare muon-to-electron particle transformation. Sarah Demers, chair of the physics department at Yale University and a participant in the Mu2e work, noted that AI helps optimize experimental setups by handling variable controls like magnetic fields and target placement. 

While engaging with AI for specific research computations and data analysis on experiments like ATLAS at the Large Hadron Collider, Demers holds significant reservations about large language models. She points to intellectual property concerns, noting that popular models train on others’ writing without proper credit, creating a moral hazard regarding academic attribution. Leading an effort through the American Physical Society to draft an AI policy statement, Demers emphasizes that practicing physicists must validate any information produced by models to maintain scientific progress rather than adding noise to the system. 

Demers distinguishes between using AI as a tool for advanced data tasks and relying on it as a substitute for human thought or communication. She avoids using LLMs for personal tasks like drafting emails, arguing that navigating difficult messages is part of solving the root challenge rather than something to bypass.
