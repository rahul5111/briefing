Whole-genome duplication, or polyploidy, is the single most radical mutation an organism can experience in a single generation, leaving an individual with three, four, or more sets of chromosomes instead of the standard two. While scientists suspect that most polyploidy events are fatal cell-division errors or lead to early spontaneous abortions in humans, those that stabilize can drive profound evolutionary change. 

Advances in whole-genome sequencing are revealing that genome duplication is far more common across the tree of life than previously understood. All living seed plants have experienced at least one ancient whole-genome duplication, and ancient duplications appear in the genetic histories of barnacles, insects, trout, and arachnids. 

Researchers studying this phenomenon include evolutionary biologists Pamela and Douglas Soltis, who tracked a polyploidy event sparked when European goatsbeard plants were brought to eastern Washington in the mid-1920s, hybridized, and produced new species with 24 chromosomes—double the original count. In the immediate aftermath, the plants immediately began altering, discarding, and retaining surplus genes. 

Scientists are also examining how species survive the initial cellular shock. University of Iowa evolutionary biologist Maurine Neiman recently dated a genome duplication in the tiny New Zealand snail Potamopyrgus antipodarum, found along the shores of Lake Alexandrina, to less than a million years ago. This recent timeline allows researchers a close view of how the snail manages its heavier genomic load. Some researchers also suspect a link between full-scale environmental cataclysms, such as the asteroid collision that ended the dinosaurs 66 million years ago, and clusters of genome duplications, though a 2026 paper proposing this connection remains controversial. 

As Douglas Soltis described the phenomenon, polyploidy is like a baseball hitter that strikes out often but occasionally hits a home run.
