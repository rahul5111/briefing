═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Long before modern web platforms standardized computing, developers turned to email as a universal interface to reach distant computers without building dedicated apps for every operating system. In 1985, Jack Dongarra and Eric Grosse launched Netlib to distribute mathematical software to researchers over ARPANET. Users could send an email requesting specific numerical routines, and Netlib’s server would parse the text, package the code, and reply with the download. Within two years of its launch, Netlib was handling nearly 4,000 software downloads a month.

This model of using email to control machines expanded through the decades. In the early 1990s, FTPmail allowed users to fetch files from FTP servers, MCI Mail enabled faxing, and IMDb founder Alan Jay used email to let users query databases for cast lists and filmographies. The approach endured as software evolved. Applications such as Remember the Milk, Instapaper, Readwise, and WordPress integrated email workflows to let users manage tasks, forward articles, and publish content directly from their inboxes. Beyond acting as an interface, email also serves as a universal online passport and identity system, powering automated bug-fix notifications and streamlining registrations through methods like Japan's Karamail blank-email signups.
