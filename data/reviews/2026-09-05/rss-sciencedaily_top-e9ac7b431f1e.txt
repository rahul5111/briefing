═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

In August 2026, a giant flat-topped iceberg measuring just over 76 square kilometers broke away from Greenland’s Petermann Glacier, marking the largest Arctic glacier calving event since 2020. The separation was first identified on August 4 by University of Ottawa doctoral student Adam Garbo using imagery from the European Space Agency's Sentinel-1 mission. Researchers had been monitoring different rifts across the floating ice tongue, but as Garbo stated, what surprised us was that the calving instead followed a different fracture, producing a smaller ice island than we had originally anticipated. Additional rifts on the glacier are expected to eventually release new ice islands measuring roughly 94 square kilometers and 84 square kilometers. After breaking free, the new iceberg moved down Petermann Fjord toward Nares Strait at an average speed of about 3 kilometers per day during its first week. Estimated to be less than 150 meters thick, the berg subsequently collided with Joe Island on August 23 and August 24, an encounter captured by Landsat 9. Although a 2010 Petermann ice island split into two pieces after striking the same rocky outcrop, this recent berg survived the impact without breaking apart. As Garbo noted, we were certainly watching closely as it interacted with Joe Island and were impressed that it survived the interaction without further fragmentation. Winds and surface currents subsequently carried the intact ice island out of the fjord and southwest through Nares Strait, where it will eventually fracture into smaller pieces due to tides, winds, ocean currents, and melting.
