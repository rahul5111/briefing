═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers at the University of Toulouse-CNRS and the University of Leeds have developed a mathematical framework showing that high levels of agreement or unanimity among decision-making groups are not always reliable indicators of expertise and trustworthiness. Published in the Journal of the Royal Society Interface, the study found that when conformity bias is factored into realistic scenarios, groups that appear entirely confident can actually be less trustworthy than those exhibiting some dissent. 

First author Aurèle Boussard explained that while a purely rational view might lead one to assume that increased agreement means a group is more likely to be right, reality is more complex. As Boussard stated, "While reading a previous paper, we realized that a paradoxical—yet intuitive—result could emerge from Bayesian decision-making. In this framework, individuals balance private and social information in an optimal way. From that, it reasonably follows that the more individuals agree, the more likely the group is to be right. However, we found situations leading to the opposite conclusion, in which the more confident a group seems, the less trustworthy it is."

To cover plausible real-life group decisions, the researchers used 16 model cases varying conformity formulations and decision complexities. Their findings indicate that high agreement is a sign of trustworthiness primarily when decisions are fairly simple. For more difficult choices, the presence of dissent suggests that members were less influenced by conformity bias, making disagreement a better hint of reliability when evaluating collective decisions from the outside.
