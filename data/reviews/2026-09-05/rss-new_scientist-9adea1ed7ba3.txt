═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

AI company Anthropic has used a group of AI agents to create a formalised proof of Fermat’s last theorem in just 11 days, confirming the correctness of the human-found proof proposed in the 1990s by Andrew Wiles. Fermat posed the puzzle in the 17th century, and it remained unproven until Wiles succeeded in 1995 after working on it for seven years in secret, later requiring a year to fix a flaw in the original 1993 breakthrough with collaborator Richard Taylor. Anthropic’s Claude model worked continuously and autonomously for 11 days, with numerous separate AI agents tackling smaller chunks of the theorem while human experts occasionally issued high-level instructions to keep the work on track. Success came after the company began using a tool designed for human mathematical collaboration called Prove2Me, which helped the agents track their work and decide on next tasks. The resulting formalisation runs to 13 million lines of the programming language Lean and covers roughly 29,500 intermediate theorems, making the proof over five times the size of all previous work stored in the central Mathlib repository and the largest Lean proof ever written. Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College London, who had been leading a separate five-year project to formalise Wiles and Taylor’s 100-page proof in Lean before Anthropic’s announcement overtook the effort, stated that the company’s proof leaves no assumptions other than the axioms of mathematics. Buzzard noted in a statement published by Anthropic that along the way they saw autoformalisation of algebra, harmonic analysis, geometry, and number theory, demonstrating that AI autoformalisation artefacts are now robust enough to be built upon. If the automatic formalisation of the theorem is possible now, Buzzard added, a big step has been taken towards the automatic formalisation of modern mathematical literature.
