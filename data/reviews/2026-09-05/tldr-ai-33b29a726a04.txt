═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Large American companies typically operate with hundreds or thousands of software products, ranging from systems of record like SAP and Workday to vertical SaaS applications, spreadsheets, and scripts, often without fully knowing what is used or paid for. While artificial intelligence makes it easier and cheaper to build tools without writing code, this capability confronts the reality that most people are not tool-builders focused on changing how their jobs are done, but are instead focused on clients, cases, or sales. 

Enterprise AI adoption over the past three years reflects this friction. When companies give employees access to tools like Copilot, ChatGPT, or Claude, a small group uses them heavily, a larger set uses them occasionally, and many do not use them at all. This dynamic mirrors the introduction of personal computers with Lotus 123 in 1983 or web browsers in 1997, where widespread tool access did not automatically transform structural processes like invoice processing or supply chain management. Companies currently rely on pilots to automate previously unautomatable processes, with roughly half of these trials succeeding. However, as the source notes, giving everyone ChatGPT scales theoretically, but "most people aren't really finding ways to use it."

Software adoption exists on a spectrum from institutionalized systems managed centrally for compliance and audit to improvised, bottom-up solutions built in spreadsheets and email. AI expands both existing applications and freeform spaces like chatbots, creating new choices and shifting thresholds rather than eliminating the need for structured company decisions. Transforming how a company operates around a generational technology requires addressing three distinct categories of questions: how to buy, build, and deploy the technology; how deeply it changes operations; and whether it introduces new economic challenges, competitive pressures, or existential threats.
