═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

A new study led by the University of Jyväskylä in Finland has evaluated an ecological threshold for clearcutting in boreal forests to protect declining biodiversity. Published in the Journal of Applied Ecology, the research analyzed national bird-monitoring program data from southern and central Finland covering the years 2006 to 2020 alongside European maps of forest disturbances. 

Researchers found that the abundance of both common forest species and those specialized in old-growth forests was lower in landscapes where large areas of clearcutting had accumulated over a 20-year period. To establish a safe operating space where bird populations associated with old-growth forests can remain stable or recover, the study determined that the proportion of clearcutting should not exceed approximately ten percent of the forest landscape over a ten-year period. 

Senior Researcher Rémi Duflot stated that this threshold corresponds to maintaining a forest rotation period of at least 90 years on average, or excluding approximately one-fifth of the forest area from clearcutting management. The findings provide decision-makers, forest owners, and forestry stakeholders with a concrete metric for planning ecologically sustainable forest management and reconciling forest use with the protection of biodiversity. 

The researchers noted that ecological thresholds may vary by region and among different groups of organisms, such as lichens and mosses, meaning these results should be supplemented by further studies on other species before broad thresholds are adopted.

═══ 04_AUDIO_REWRITE ═══

Researchers at the University of Jyväskylä, in Finland, have evaluated an ecological threshold for clearcutting in boreal forests. The goal is to protect declining biodiversity. The study was published in the Journal of Applied Ecology. Researchers analyzed national bird-monitoring program data from southern and central Finland. The data covered the years two thousand six to two thousand twenty. They also used European maps of forest disturbances.

Researchers found that bird abundance was lower in landscapes with accumulated clearcutting. This applied to both common forest species and species specialized in old-growth forests. The accumulation occurred over a twenty-year period. The study determined a safe operating space for bird populations. Old-growth forest birds can remain stable or recover in this space. The proportion of clearcutting should not exceed approximately ten percent of the forest landscape over a ten-year period.

Senior Researcher, Rémi Duflot, stated that this threshold corresponds to maintaining a forest rotation period. That period must be at least ninety years on average. Another option is excluding approximately one-fifth of the forest area from clearcutting management. The findings provide decision-makers, forest owners, and forestry stakeholders with a concrete metric. They can use it for planning ecologically sustainable forest management. It helps reconcile forest use with the protection of biodiversity.

The researchers noted that ecological thresholds may vary by region. They may also vary among different groups of organisms like lichens and mosses. These results should be supplemented by further studies on other species. That must happen before broad thresholds are adopted.
