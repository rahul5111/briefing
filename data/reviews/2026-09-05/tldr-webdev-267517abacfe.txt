═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

While artificial intelligence progress races ahead in knowledge work, physical robotics remains largely confined to test facilities and demo videos, where a successful task may represent one out of 100 attempts. Developing broadly capable humanoid robots requires overcoming numerous foundational technical challenges. 

Robot hands and manipulators remain a pale imitation of the human hand, which features roughly two dozen degrees of freedom and approximately 17,000 tactile sensors. While some robotic counterparts match individual metrics, none combine flexibility, sensitivity, strength, and reliability. Additionally, computer vision struggles to make sense of complicated visual scenes, such as picking objects from crowded environments or determining safe footing. 

True autonomy demands that robots break down complex tasks like cooking or engine repair, plan sequences of motions, and instantly react to surprises like a stuck bolt or a darting child. Unlike virtual AI agents that operate in static isolation, robots must coordinate in changing environments while burdened by slow movement speeds. General-purpose humanoid robots also struggle to combine strength with manageable weight, generating more heat and depleting batteries faster. A human-sized robot generates about twice as much heat as a person while lacking the human body's circulation and perspiration systems for heat dissipation. 

Form factor debates persist between wheels and legs, while safety considerations multiply because robots move constantly and lack the ability to simply pull over like self-driving cars. Frequent breakdowns stem from large numbers of finicky moving parts, contrasting with the human body's self-repair capabilities. Furthermore, achieving reliable generalization across countless edge cases in homes and businesses will prove extremely difficult, compounded by the challenge of incorporating massive computing power directly into the robot.
