═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Researchers from Kyoto University, Hiroshima University, and Nihon University used Earth and its ionosphere as a planet-sized detector to search for ultralight axions and dark photons, two leading candidates for dark matter. The team developed a new theoretical framework incorporating atmospheric electrical conductivity, allowing them to make reliable predictions up to about 30 Hz by utilizing the Earth-ionosphere cavity as a natural resonator that amplifies signals near 8 Hz. Applying this model to a decade of geomagnetic measurements collected between 2012 and 2022 by the British Geological Survey's Eskdalemuir Observatory, the team searched for steady signals within a narrow frequency range while filtering out artificial noise. The approach placed new limits on how strongly axions can interact with light, achieving results about 100 times tighter than previous best ground-based experiments. The dark photon search also uncovered several intriguing signal candidates that could potentially originate from dark matter, though their exact source remains unexplained and unconfirmed. As corresponding author Atsushi Taruya stated, we asked ourselves whether we could use the Earth itself as a giant detector in the search.
