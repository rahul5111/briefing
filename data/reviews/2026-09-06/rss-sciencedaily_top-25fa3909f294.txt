Indonesia’s peat fires are surging early in the 2026 fire season as a strengthening El Nino and severe drought dry out wetlands that can burn underground for months. On September 1, 2026, the MODIS instrument aboard NASA's Aqua satellite captured images of the fires, while Indonesia's SiPongi monitoring platform counted 946 hotspots on August 31, 2026. Peat fires are exceptionally destructive because they produce three times more fine particulate matter than typical tropical forest fires, along with five times more sulfur dioxide, three times more organic carbon, and twice as much methane and carbon monoxide. Indonesia contains about 36 percent of the world's tropical peatlands, which can dry and ignite during severe droughts. NOAA assessed El Nino as present and strengthening in August 2026, a climate pattern that reduces rainfall and is occurring alongside a positive phase of the Indian Ocean Dipole. Robert Field, a Columbia University researcher, noted that fire activity is tracking sharply upward, similar to the severe 2015 season when fires emitted 1.75 billion tons of greenhouse gas equivalents over three months. By September 2, the 2026 fires had already released roughly 10 percent of that amount. Data from the Indonesian meteorological agency showed that about 90 percent of the country received little or no rainfall in early August 2026, allowing flames to enter peat deposits in Kalimantan, Sumatra, and Papua. Satellite instruments like MODIS and VIIRS can struggle to detect these fires when they are hidden by thick smoke or burning underground. The heavy smoke has already disrupted daily life, prompting some schools to switch to remote learning, closing nine national parks, and delaying several flights.
