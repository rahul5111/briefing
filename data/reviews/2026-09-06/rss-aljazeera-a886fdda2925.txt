═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Communities across the United States are increasingly turning to potlucks and shared meals to combat rising loneliness and social division. On July 5, an initiative called America’s Potluck, led by the America250 Utah Commission, brought people together across all 50 states to mark the country’s semiquincentennial. The event included more than 1,700 participating communities, such as the Trenton, New Jersey neighbourhood of Hiltonia, where about 25 residents gathered for a neighbourhood potluck organized with the help of Revolution NJ. 

The push to revive communal dining comes as Americans increasingly dine alone. Data from 2023 shows that about one in four adults reported eating all their meals alone the previous day, marking a 53 percent increase since 2003. Research highlights the social toll and benefits of these dining habits. The 2025 World Happiness Report found a strong link between sharing meals and higher subjective well-being, while a report from the Foundation for Social Connection points to food as a force for building social ties. 

Organizations across the country are capitalizing on this dynamic. In Maine, the group Community Plate has hosted more than 50 gatherings since its first supper in Lubec in 2023. Surveys of nearly 400 Community Plate participants found that 96 percent made ties with someone they did not know well, and 88 percent felt more connected to their community afterward. 

Food historians note that potlucks have long served as an ancient social form for pooling community resources and creating a sense of common care outside of family structures. As Karl Schatz, cofounder of Community Plate, described the dynamic of sharing dishes, “When people bring food… and you’re feeding each other, there’s this simultaneous giving and receiving that’s taking place. That creates a sense of both helping and being helped.”
