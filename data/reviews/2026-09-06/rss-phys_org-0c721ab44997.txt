═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

Scientists have synthesized record-long single-atom copper chains by applying extreme pressure to a common blue pigment, offering a potential pathway to microscopic molecular wires as traditional silicon-based chips approach their physical limits. Researchers used copper phthalocyanine crystals, the base for phthalo blue, and subjected them to pressures exceeding 21 gigapascals, which is roughly 200,000 times normal atmospheric pressure, inside a diamond anvil cell. This high-pressure solid-state reaction bypassed the limitations of traditional liquid-solution methods, which typically halt synthesis when stabilizing chemical wrappers become insoluble and yield chains fewer than 10 atoms long. The resulting structure features more than 4,000 copper atoms strung into a single chain stretching for micrometers, making it two to three orders of magnitude longer than the previous record of 28 atoms. Each nanowire cable forms a three-layer structure consisting of a copper atomic core, a conductive ring-like frame of carbon and nitrogen atoms, and an outer protective sp3-carbon network sheath that locks the chain into a stable line. Computer models and measurements published in Science showed that electricity flows more than 10 times faster along the wire than sideways in real-world samples, with the surrounding carbon and nitrogen framework rather than the weakly bonded copper atoms carrying the current. While the wires proved stable in air and survived hours in an acidic solution under harsh ultrasonic vibrations, the requirement of extreme pressure presents a bottleneck that could hinder industrial-scale production.
