═══ 01_DRAFT ═══

University of Montreal researcher Tobi Matzek and colleagues have found that lucid dreams can be induced in some people using cognitive and multisensory stimulation. The team recruited 60 healthy adults who had experienced at least one lucid dream previously. Participants visited labs in Italy, Canada, or the Netherlands on two mornings to nap for up to 2.5 hours. Before each nap, they underwent half an hour of lucidity training while wearing a ZMax EEG sleep-tracking headband that delivered red-light flashes, a short melody, and soft forehead vibrations. A soundtrack guided them to focus on these sensations and notice differences compared to normal wakefulness. Team member Claudia Picard-Deland notes this may train people to pay closer attention to sensory changes and recognize odd or inconsistent dream experiences. During one nap per participant, the sensory stimuli were repeated during rapid eye movement sleep. Participants were taught to signal a lucid dream by moving their eyes from left to right. Just over half of the participants gave this signal while headset readings confirmed they were asleep, and they reported experiencing a lucid dream after waking. Re-applying the sensory stimulation during REM sleep did not make lucid dreams more likely to occur, but the gap between the first and last eye signals indicated the dreams lasted about twice as long in that condition. Matzek points out that longer lucid dreams could be useful for boosting creativity, problem-solving, and treating nightmares in people with post-traumatic stress disorder. However, about 55 percent of people have ever experienced lucid dreams, and it remains unclear if the approach works for individuals who have never had one. Kristoffer Appel at the Institute of Sleep and Dream Technologies in Hamburg, Germany, calls the results remarkable, stating they suggest lucidity is far more trainable on short notice than its usual spontaneous and rare reputation.
